# The Routine Run method

**Your head is for decisions, not for remembering the order of steps.**

*Start with what you can do now. Then keep what should happen again.*

A routine is the way something is done. A run is one time it is done. The app holds the
order, the timing and what happened last time. You do the steps, make the decisions, and
after each run say what the routine should keep. Run by run, the routine becomes what the
work actually is.

A checklist is classical music: a score, played as written, the same every time. Routine Run
is jazz: a tune everybody knows, played the way today needs, and what worked tonight can join
the tune. *Let your flow fly.*

If no planner has ever stuck for you, start with [the method in one afternoon](https://routinerun.com/between). To see it beside the tools you already use, [How it compares](compare.md).

Each principle below has the same parts: the idea, an example, what the app does today, what
research has found, and what we don't claim. The research is about people in general. None of
it is a study of this app.

## The method in eighteen lines

1. [The machine holds, you decide.](#the-machine-holds) Hand over the remembering, keep the deciding.
2. [One routine, many runs.](#one-routine-many-runs) The way it is done, and each time it was done, are two things.
3. [Built by running.](#built-by-running) Each run tries the routine; Keep and Only here decide what the next run starts with.
4. [Each run takes its own path.](#runs-take-their-own-path) The routine gives a default order; a run takes the order today allows.
5. [Kept once, reaches every run.](#kept-once-reaches-every-run) A kept change reaches every run still going, for the steps it has not reached yet.
6. [Only what is going.](#only-what-is-going) About four things fit in mind, so only what is active is in sight.
7. [One step at a time.](#one-step-at-a-time) The step in hand, not the list.
8. [Steps, not checkboxes.](#steps-not-checkboxes) No ticks, no scores. A follow-up is a step.
9. [If this, then that step.](#if-then) Triggers start runs and move steps; steps can act by themselves.
10. [Check-ins where there is no date.](#check-ins) A question that asks until it is answered.
11. [Dates that float.](#dates-that-float) Moments counted from an event, so they move when it moves.
12. [Put off, not dropped.](#put-off-not-dropped) Later, At the end, a time, a place, or Decide later.
13. [On the go, and time set aside.](#on-the-go-and-time-set-aside) Catch with one tap; sort when there is time.
14. [Filter what arrives.](#filter-what-arrives) Gather, don't silence.
15. [The tune and the playing.](#the-tune-and-the-playing) Structure plus variation, built around the conditions for flow.
16. [A guide, not an autopilot.](#a-guide-not-an-autopilot) The machine does what can be undone; you decide what cannot.
17. [Who it is for.](#who-it-is-for) People winning time back, not filling it: organising what they already do, or growing a new habit from one action.
18. [Steps become habit, or the machine's.](#the-guide-steps-back) While a routine is run, each step moves to habit or to the machine, one at a time.

<a id="the-machine-holds"></a>
## 1. The machine holds, you decide

Every process kept in memory (which step is next, what is due, what happened last time) takes
attention the work itself needs. The method hands the *holding* to the machine and leaves the
*doing* and the *deciding* with you.

**For example.** The on-call engineer at 3 a.m. does not have to remember whether the cache was
flushed. The run knows it is on step four, and says so on the wrist.

**In the app**
- The Routine holds the order. The Run holds where you are and what is due. Triggers hold when.
- A timer tells you. It does not decide you have stopped.
- Deleted things wait 30 days in Recently Deleted, and finished runs are kept.

**What the research says**
- People hand memory to notes and tools, and do more of it as demands rise
  ([Risko & Gilbert 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002)). In experiments with
  1,196 people, reminders improved performance
  ([Gilbert 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/17470218.2014.972963)).
- Offloading frees the mind only when the store is trusted
  ([Storm & Stone 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614559285)).

**What we don't claim**
- What is offloaded is remembered less
  ([Grinschgl et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/17470218211008060)). A routine will not
  teach you its list by heart.
- People who lean on automation watch it less closely
  ([Parasuraman & Manzey 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055)), so the decisions
  stay with you.

<a id="one-routine-many-runs"></a>
## 2. One routine, many runs

A routine is the way something is done. A run is one time it was done. A routine can have one
run in its life (a move) or hundreds (every morning), and several runs can be going at once,
each on its own step.

**For example.** A realtor has one routine, "Contract to close", and a run for each client,
named for the client and the address. The routine says how a deal is done. Each run says how
*this* deal is going.

**In the app**
- A Run starts from a Routine and keeps its own record: what was done, skipped, noted and
  changed.
- Several runs of one routine can go at once, each with its own name.
- Edit the routine and the runs going see the edit. A finished run keeps the routine as it
  read it.

**What the research says**
- Research on organisational routines draws the same line: the pattern people refer to, and
  each performance of it, which in turn reshapes the pattern
  ([Feldman & Pentland 2003](https://doi.org/10.2307/3556620)).
- Process software separates a template from its cases, and lets a case depart from the
  template ([van der Aalst et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.datak.2004.07.003)).

**What we don't claim**
- The list the app stores is the routine *as written*. What people actually do is always
  richer.

<a id="built-by-running"></a>
## 3. Built by running

Start with what you can do now. A routine is not planned up front and then obeyed; it begins
from the steps you already do, and it is grown: each run does what it can, whatever the run
changed gets a short answer, and what should happen again goes into the next run. Small
improvements, made by the person doing the work, every time through: kaizen, for one person's
routine.

**For example.** The first "Morning" is not the twelve steps of an ideal morning but the three
already done every day: kettle, shower, keys. A stretch joins them later. One morning it is cut to five minutes: Only
here, the day was short. The next it is cut again: Keep, five was enough. After an incident, a new step,
"check the queue depth before restarting", gets Keep.

**One step first; split it when a part needs a life of its own.** Something new starts as a
single step, and what it needs goes into that step as a list: "Pack for the trip", with the
things to take written underneath. A part becomes a step of its own only once it turns out to
need what only a step has: its own time or timer, its own place in the order, being put off on
its own, being done by the machine, or a record of its own. Until then one step with a list is
lighter than eight steps, and the runs show which lines have earned more. It is the other half
of [principle 18](#the-guide-steps-back): a step is split when a part earns it, and folded back
when it no longer needs spelling out.

**In the app**
- Anything can be changed for this run: a step's words, its duration, a new step, a note.
- Each change is marked orange and counted in the band at the top. Keep carries it up to the
  routine. Only here says it belonged to today, and the mark goes grey.
- One screen lists every unanswered change, so the count can always reach nought.
- A step holds a description, where such a list lives, and notes, values and links beside it.
  A part becomes its own step as a change in a run, answered Keep like any other.

**What the research says**
- A four-year field study found routines change through people's reflections on earlier
  rounds ([Feldman 2000](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.11.6.611.12529)).
- A meta-analysis of 46 samples found debriefs and after-action reviews improved performance by about 25% on average ([Tannenbaum & Cerasoli 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720812448394)), and more
  when a record helps people remember
  ([Keiser & Arthur 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000821)).
- Kaizen, Toyota's practice of small improvements made by the people who do the work, treats
  each written standard as a hypothesis every doing tests (Imai 1986;
  [Spear & Bowen 1999](https://hbr.org/1999/09/decoding-the-dna-of-the-toyota-production-system)).

**What we don't claim**
- Not "25% better". That figure is for debriefs in general, across teams and individuals, often in training.

<a id="runs-take-their-own-path"></a>
## 4. Each run takes its own path

A routine gives a default order, not a script. Most steps do not depend on each other's order,
so a run may take them in the order today allows. Where order truly matters, the routine says
so, and there it holds.

**For example.** The routine says keys, wallet, bins, lights. Today the bins go first because
the lorry is in the street, and nothing is wrong with the run. A surgical time-out, though,
comes before the first incision, always.

**In the app**
- Later and At the end move a step for this run, and steps can be reordered or added for this run
  only.
- Every new run starts from the routine's order.
- Where order matters, the author can switch off the buttons that move a step.

**What the research says**
- In invoice processing at four organisations, one routine produced hundreds of different
  patterns over five months ([Pentland et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.1110.0624)).
- Case-handling software focuses on what can be done rather than what must be done
  ([van der Aalst et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.datak.2004.07.003)).

**What we don't claim**
- Not "order rarely matters". Checklists fail when items are taken out of turn
  ([Degani & Wiener 1993](https://doi.org/10.1177/001872089303500209)).
- Freedom is not free: every switch costs a little, so a run still shows one step in hand.

<a id="kept-once-reaches-every-run"></a>
## 5. Kept once, reaches every run

When one run finds something better and it is kept, every run of that routine still going
picks it up, for the steps it has not reached yet. What a run has already done, or said for
itself, is never rewritten.

**For example.** On one deal a realtor learns the county's inspectors want the permit history
in advance, adds a step to send it, and answers Keep. The other deals not yet at inspection now
have the step. The ones past it are left alone.

**In the app**
- A run reads its steps from the routine as it is now and stores only how it differs, so a
  kept change reaches every run still going.
- A run's own changes win for that run.
- A finished run keeps the routine as it read it.

**What the research says**
- Knowledge built into tools and routines travels more easily than knowledge held by people
  ([Argote & Ingram 2000](https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.2000.2893)).
- In 36 pizza stores, lessons spread to stores with the same owner, and learning by doing
  faded fast ([Darr et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.41.11.1750)).
- Moving a running case onto a changed process carelessly can skip or repeat work
  ([van der Aalst 2001](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1011409408711)).

**What we don't claim**
- Not "every run changes everywhere at once". Only steps a run has not reached, and never over
  what that run said for itself.

<a id="only-what-is-going"></a>
## 6. Only what is going

People hold far fewer things in mind than they think, and every open thing costs attention
even while nobody works on it. So only what is going stays in view, and you choose what that
is.

**For example.** Four runs are going on a Tuesday afternoon, and two are being worked on. The
other two are swiped off the Lock Screen. They keep their place and come back when picked up.

**In the app**
- A going run sits on the Lock Screen and in the Dynamic Island as a Live Activity. The system
  shows only a few at a time.
- The bar at the foot of the app answers one question: what is going.
- An Inbox waiting for more leaves the bar until something arrives.

**What the research says**
- Adults hold about four chunks in the focus of attention, not seven or nine
  ([Cowan 2001](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922)).
- Switching tasks costs time and errors, and having several tasks in play slows each one even
  without a switch ([Monsell 2003](https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613%2803%2900028-7)).
- An unfinished task leaves attention behind that lowers performance on the next
  ([Leroy 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002)).

**What we don't claim**
- Not "multitasking damages your brain". Those findings largely failed to replicate
  ([Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein 2017](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-017-1408-4)).

<a id="one-step-at-a-time"></a>
## 7. One step at a time

Inside a run you see the step you are on, not the list. A whole list is load that does
nothing for the step in hand, and it tempts you to pick what is quickest to close instead of
what comes next.

**For example.** Leaving the house has eight steps. With the list on screen, the eye goes to
"take the rubbish out" while "keys" waits. With one step in hand, the next step is keys.

**In the app**
- A run's page shows the step it is on. The list of steps is one tap away.
- Moving on is deliberate: Next, or Later and At the end to put the step elsewhere on purpose.
- Put the phone away mid-step, and the run opens on the same step.

**What the research says**
- Asked to carry one of two buckets, people picked the nearer one and carried it further, to
  finish sooner ([Rosenbaum et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614532657)).
- People chose urgent tasks that paid less over important ones that paid more
  ([Zhu, Yang & Hsee 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucy008)).
- On flight decks, checklist items get skipped when the crew is interrupted
  ([Degani & Wiener 1993](https://doi.org/10.1177/001872089303500209)).

**What we don't claim**
- Not that the whole list is bad. For a routine you know cold, doing it from memory and
  checking at the end fits better, and the list allows that.

<a id="steps-not-checkboxes"></a>
## 8. Steps, not checkboxes

The unit of work is a step, not a box to tick. A box needs a measure of done, much of what
matters has none, and a list of boxes rewards ticking. So a step is done by moving on, it
fills with context, and a follow-up is another step with a when.

**Done elsewhere is done.** The job is to hand the remembering to the machine, not to tick a
box and feel good about it. If you finish something that is also a step somewhere in a routine,
you do not go looking for where it lives, and you do not try to make it the current step. You
carry on with the step in hand. When the finished step comes up, you walk past it. The record
may then say less than a report would want, and that is the trade: the method is after flow,
not after managing a process for its own sake. So the same step in several routines or runs is
fine. Walking past a duplicate is cheaper than keeping the system tidy.

**A score, if there is one, comes from outside.** A score is not forbidden. A score built on
your own decisions is: ten tasks made and ten ticked rewards empty tasks, rushed work and easy
tasks picked over the ones that matter. A score can only count what effort aimed at the number
cannot produce. The test is one question: can this number go up without the real thing getting
better? If it can, it is not a score. So the grader is outside you: a release that passed
review, a customer who replied or paid, a milestone whose end somebody or something else
confirms. What counts as done is decided once, when the milestone is set, so nobody has to
judge success each time. And a result counts when it is above your own recent level, so
repeating the same one stops paying.

**For example.** By five o'clock nine of twelve items are ticked: emails, a receipt, a
haircut. The three left are the proposal, the hard conversation and the tax question.

**In the app**
- No score, no streak, no percentage.
- A step already done elsewhere is walked past with Next when it comes up. Nothing asks where
  it was done.

- A step collects notes and values while in hand, and the next steps read them.
- The one number a run has counts questions to answer, never achievements.
- If you want a count, a value on a step can keep it, by hand or by an automation. It never
  becomes a streak.

**What the research says**
- A streak is a result, not a goal: in a well-built process it happens by itself. People with
  high self-control reach good outcomes mostly through habits, not effortful resistance
  ([Galla & Duckworth 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000026)).
- In two years of emergency-department data, doctors under heavy load picked easier cases,
  which raised throughput then and lowered productivity over time
  ([KC et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3419)).
- A number that becomes the goal stops describing the work
  ([Campbell 1979](https://doi.org/10.1016/0149-7189%2879%2990048-X)).
- A specific plan for an unfinished goal quieted it much as finishing would
  ([Masicampo & Baumeister 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192)).
- Reward systems often pay for one behaviour while hoping for another, and people do what is
  paid ([Kerr 1975](https://doi.org/10.5465/255378)).
- Across 128 experiments, tangible rewards for doing a task lowered interest in it, while
  positive, informative feedback raised it
  ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)).

**What we don't claim**
- Not "goals are harmful". Only that a list of outcomes is a poor unit for work still being
  learned.
- A run's record is not an audit. A step done elsewhere and walked past shows as passed in the
  run, not where it was really done.
- We don't claim a score helps. Only that one built on your own ticks does harm, and that one
  which could help would have to be graded from outside.
- *Atomic Habits* recommends a habit tracker and "never miss twice"
  ([Clear 2018](https://jamesclear.com/atomic-habits)). The method keeps its one-habit-at-a-time
  idea and leaves streaks out: the run's record is the tracker, without a score. A chain that
  must not break makes the habit the master. Missing a single day did not materially affect
  habit formation ([Lally et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)), and a formed habit runs on even after its goal has changed
  ([Wood & Rünger 2016](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-122414-033417)).

<a id="if-then"></a>
## 9. If this, then that step

When something happens (a time, a place, a Focus, a message from another system), a run
starts or a step moves, and a step may do its own part. You decide the "if" once, and stop
carrying it.

**For example.** Arriving at the gym starts "Workout". An alert from monitoring starts the
runbook on the engineer's wrist. "Into deep work" reaches its third step and runs a shortcut
that turns on a Focus.

**In the app**
- A Trigger starts a run or moves a step: a time, a place, a Focus, a workout, another app, an
  event from outside.
- An Action is what a step does by itself: run a shortcut, set a Home scene, write a value.
- A trigger can carry conditions, such as weekdays only.

**What the research says**
- A plan tying a situation to a response is carried out far more often than a goal alone. A
  meta-analysis of 94 tests found d = 0.65
  ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601%2806%2938002-1)), and one of 642
  tests found larger effects for plans written as if-then
  ([Sheeran et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563)).
- Formed habits are set off by context, not by a goal
  ([Wood & Neal 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.114.4.843)).

**What we don't claim**
- Not "a habit in 21 days". One study found a median of 66 days to reach near-full automaticity, ranging from 18 to 254
  ([Lally et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)).

<a id="check-ins"></a>
## 10. Check-ins where there is no date

Some things have no moment to wait for: the appraisal, the parcel, the reply. Nothing tells
you it has happened, so you keep checking in your head. A check-in asks a short question every
so often, and the answer ends the asking and moves the run.

**For example.** "Has the appraisal arrived?" every morning at nine. Not yet, until the day it
is Yes, and Yes moves the run on.

**In the app**
- A check-in is a trigger with a question, on a run or a step, asking every so many minutes,
  hours or days, with its answers beside Not yet.
- Each new card replaces the last. An answer takes its cards away.
- While a step's check-in waits, the step goes to the end, so the run is not stuck on it.

**What the research says**
- Holding an intention to act on a future event slowed people's other work before the event
  came ([Smith 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12776746/)).
- In randomised trials at three banks, reminders raised savings by about 6%
  ([Karlan et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2296)).

**What we don't claim**
- Asking again wears the signal down: in one clinical study, each extra reminder per visit
  lowered the chance of acting on one by 30%
  ([Ancker et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-017-0430-8)). So an answer ends it.
- No study looks at exactly this case. The link is our inference.

<a id="dates-that-float"></a>
## 11. Dates that float

Many deadlines are not dates: "ten days after the offer is accepted", "the evening before the
flight". The event is not known when the routine is written, and it moves. So a step's moment
can be counted from a date the run carries, and moves when that date moves.

**For example.** Check in online 24 hours before the flight, charge the headphones the evening
before, expense the taxi two days after landing. Move the flight and all three move. Or a
presentation at 15:00: research three hours before, prepare what you need two hours before,
breathe and put your thoughts in order half an hour before, turn Zoom on and open what you need
five minutes before. Laid out at fixed times, every piece has to be moved by hand when the
presentation moves. Hung on the event, the whole plan moves with it.

**In the app**
- A step can be due by a time, or start at a time, counted from a date the run or routine
  carries, plus or minus an offset.
- Anything with a date is mentioned a day before, and when late it asks rather than being
  skipped.
- These deadlines can go into your calendar or Reminders, and are written again when the date
  moves.
- Alerts on your own calendar event can drive a run: start a routine, bring the run to the step
  for that stretch, or start another routine. They are counted from the event, so moving the
  event moves the plan. This needs an iPhone on iOS 27 with the Routine Run shortcut set up.

**What the research says**
- The critical path method made every activity hang on what it depends on, so a change
  upstream moves everything after it
  ([Kelley & Walker 1959](https://doi.org/10.1145/1460299.1460318)).
- Students predicted their thesis would take about 34 days. It took about 56, longer than
  their worst case ([Buehler et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366)).

**What we don't claim**
- Counting from an event matches how people talk about time. We don't claim it makes anybody
  perform better.

<a id="put-off-not-dropped"></a>
## 12. Put off, not dropped

A step that cannot be done now is not skipped and not left hanging. It is put somewhere it
will come back from, and there are as many ways to say where as there are real reasons for
not now.

**For example.** No milk for "Make coffee": Later puts it after "go to the shop". "Sign the
lease" waits on the landlord: At the end. A step you cannot decide about while walking: Decide
later.

**In the app**
- From Not now… on the step, Later moves it one place on and At the end after everything
  else. Pause holds the whole run.
- A step can be put off to a time or a place, and comes back then or there.
- Decide later moves it to the end with a quiet question on it.
- Nothing is deleted. The record says what happened.

**What the research says**
- Intentions tied to an event are remembered more reliably than ones tied to a clock time
  ([McDaniel & Einstein 2000](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.775)).
- A short plan for how and when to come back to an interrupted task removed the attention it
  left behind ([Leroy & Glomb 2018](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2017.1184)).

**What we don't claim**
- A bare "later" is not a plan. The research supports putting off with a when or a where.
- Hard choices are the ones most often put off for good
  ([Tversky & Shafir 1992](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1992.tb00047.x)), so Decide
  later's question stays in view until answered.

<a id="on-the-go-and-time-set-aside"></a>
## 13. On the go, and time set aside

On the go, the only job is to catch what comes up and keep moving: one tap, no decision. In
time set aside, what was caught gets sorted. Mixing the two means deciding badly on the
pavement, or not catching things at all.

**For example.** Walking to a meeting, you hear of an article and a supplier. Each goes to
Inbox with one tap. On Sunday evening the Inbox run is walked like any other, one item at a
time.

**In the app**
- A routine can stay open: out of steps, it waits for more. Inbox, To read and To watch come
  this way, and whatever is sent to them from the share sheet or the wrist lands as a step.
- What arrives is answered Only here as it arrives, and the list can say once a day how much
  is waiting.

**What the research says**
- Getting Things Done separates capturing from clarifying, with a weekly review
  ([Allen 2001](https://gettingthingsdone.com/)).
- Batching phone notifications three times a day lowered stress
  ([Fitz et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016)), and so did checking email
  three times a day ([Kushlev & Dunn 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.11.005)).

**What we don't claim**
- We found no controlled study showing that deciding later beats deciding on the spot.

<a id="filter-what-arrives"></a>
## 14. Filter what arrives

The cheapest interruption is the one that never arrives. Ignoring a notification still costs
attention, so the method narrows what can reach you to what this part of the day is for, and
gathers the rest.

**For example.** With a work Focus on, personal messages wait and the deploy runbook's card
still comes through. On call at night, an alert that can wait waits for the morning.

**In the app**
- A Focus filter tells the iPhone which routines' cards may come through while that Focus is
  on.
- Each Focus can say which routines may start, and whether they need a yes.
- Repeats fold into one interruption.

**What the research says**
- Receiving a notification, without answering it, disrupted performance on an attention task
  ([Stothart et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100)).
- In a study of 221 people, a week with alerts on brought more self-reported inattention
  ([Kushlev, Proulx & Dunn 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858359)).
- Batching notifications lowered stress; turning them all off raised anxiety
  ([Fitz et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016)).

**What we don't claim**
- Not "your phone drains your brain". That finding failed a preregistered replication
  ([Ruiz Pardo & Minda 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0001691822002323)).
- Not "turn everything off". The evidence says that backfires.

<a id="the-tune-and-the-playing"></a>
## 15. The tune and the playing

A checklist is a score, played as written, and that is its virtue. A routine is a tune,
played a little differently each time, and what worked can join the tune. The method is built
around the conditions for flow: one clear next thing, feedback straight away, room to play.
The method teaches flow and flexibility, not obedience to a habit. "Be water", as Bruce Lee put
it: the habit does not run the mind; you choose to do what you are used to.

**For example.** A pre-flight check is a score: nobody should improvise on it. A Saturday
morning is a tune: the same shape each week, but this week the walk is longer and the call
home comes first.

**In the app**
- The routine is the tune. Each run is how it is played today: steps moved with Later and
  At the end, changed or added for this run.
- What was played well can join the tune with Keep. What was only today's gets Only here.

**What the research says**
- Organisation researchers treat improvisation as a matter of degree, never as the absence of
  structure ([Weick 1998](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.9.5.543);
  [Barrett 1998](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.9.5.605)).
- Flow comes with challenges that stretch skills without overwhelming them, clear near goals
  and immediate feedback (Nakamura & Csikszentmihalyi 2002, *Handbook of Positive
  Psychology*).

**What we don't claim**
- Not "checklists are bad". After the WHO surgical checklist came into eight hospitals, deaths
  fell from 1.5% to 0.8% ([Haynes et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa0810119)).
- Not a promise of flow. We build its conditions; we don't sell the state.

<a id="a-guide-not-an-autopilot"></a>
## 16. A guide, not an autopilot

Whether a step is done by a person, a script or a software agent, the machine does what can be
undone, and the person decides what cannot.

**For example.**
- In about 45 minutes in 2012, Knight Capital built up positions that cost it more than $460 million. The SEC found the
  firm had no written procedure requiring a second technician to review the deployment
  ([SEC 2013](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)).
- In 2017 an engineer following a playbook mistyped one input and took far more Amazon S3
  servers out than intended. The fix was a safeguard in the tool
  ([AWS 2017](https://aws.amazon.com/message/41926/)).
- In 2025 Replit's coding agent ran destructive commands on a live database during a code
  freeze, despite instructions to ask first
  ([AI Incident Database](https://incidentdatabase.ai/cite/1152/)).

**In the app**
- What can be undone is not asked about: Stop and Delete put up an undo bar.
- An event from another system can be made to wait for a yes, and a journal records who
  pressed what.
- An assistant writing a routine cannot add webhooks or Home scenes, and everything is shown
  before it is saved.

**What the research says**
- The level of automation should be chosen per function, weighing reliability against the
  cost of a wrong action ([Parasuraman et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1109/3468.844354)).
- Across 18 experiments, more automation meant easier routine work and worse performance when
  it failed ([Onnasch et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720813501549)).

**What we don't claim**
- Pressing yes is not, by itself, control: people over-trust automated advice
  ([Goddard et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1136/amiajnl-2011-000089)). So questions stay
  rare.

<a id="who-it-is-for"></a>
## 17. Who it is for

There are two ways to be serious about time: fill it well, or win it back. Routine Run is for the second, and there are two ways in. One is organising what already fills your days, so the evening becomes free. The other is a new habit, built the Kaizen way: plan one action, run it, and add to it in the runs until it has become a routine.

**For example.** The on-call engineer who does not want to learn the runbook by heart. The
realtor with thirty clients who wants each deal where it is without four apps open. The parent
whose mornings run on the same twelve steps, and who would rather give the attention to the children. Somebody taking up running who plans one step, shoes on and out of the door, and adds the warm-up and the route in later runs. Somebody who has tried every planner and decided the fault was theirs, when the twelve boxes at once and the streak that broke were design decisions made for somebody else.

**In the app**
- Not a planner that lays out your day. It adds to the calendar and Reminders you already use,
  and does what they cannot: the walk from one thing to the next, which none of them holds.
- A new habit starts as a routine of one step. A run can add the next one, answered Keep, until
  the routine is the whole habit ([3](#built-by-running)).
- Not a habit tracker. A skipped step is information, not a broken chain.
- Not an autopilot. It asks before what cannot be undone.

**What the research says**
- Who it is for is a position, not a finding.
- Research on ADHD describes it as trouble doing what one knows rather than knowing what to do, and the help it describes is information and time made visible where the work happens ([Barkley](https://www.russellbarkley.org/factsheets/ADHD_EF_and_SR.pdf)).
- Highlighting a streak that broke lowered later engagement, across seven studies ([Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029)).

**What we don't claim**
- We have not studied the people who use Routine Run. Nothing here says it suits them better
  than anything else.
- Routine Run is not a medical product and does not treat or help any condition.

<a id="the-guide-steps-back"></a>
## 18. Steps become habit, or the machine's

The guide steps back. Every step starts guided: read it, then do it. Run by run, while the
routine is being run, each step can move one of two ways: to habit, where it no longer needs
spelling out, or to the machine, where the app does it. Habit matters more: a step the machine
does is one you stop practising. What cannot be undone stays with you on either road. Habit is
there to free attention, not to take the decision away; and the next habit is added once the
last one runs by itself, since adding too early can break the old one without forming the new.

**The shape of it.** The ideal process is the one you do not see. A routine starts with a few
basic steps and grows as the runs show what is missing: more steps, more instructions. Then it
peaks, with everything spelled out. After the peak what you see goes down, because steps move
into the background, to habit or to the machine. The work is still done; it just no longer asks
for your attention.

**For example.** "Fill the kettle, take the vitamins, open the blinds" were three steps. Three
months in, they are one line: "Kitchen". And "Turn on Do Not Disturb" is done by hand for a
week, then gets a shortcut you press on the step, then runs by itself as the run arrives. A
runbook for an incident that comes twice a year stays fully spelled out.

**In the app**
- To habit: start a routine with one or two steps you can do, and add the next once the first
  is done without thinking. Cut a step down to its name, move its details into a note, or fold several steps
  into one. Keep writes that into the routine. In a run's list, done steps fold into one row.
- To the machine: a step's action is set off Only when I press it, or When the run arrives. A
  step's timer can Move on by itself when it runs out.
- Every run records what was done and what was skipped.

**What the research says**
- Good teaching support is meant to be withdrawn as the learner takes over
  ([van de Pol et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-010-9127-6)).
- Guidance a beginner needs can slow down someone experienced
  ([Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4)).
- Habit apps built on reminders supported repetition but got in the way of the habit forming
  ([Stawarz et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1145/2702123.2702230)).
- *Atomic Habits* asks for a process small enough to do today, made a habit, then the next, and
  for hanging each new habit on one you already have
  ([Clear 2018](https://jamesclear.com/atomic-habits)). A popular book; it is the everyday form
  of the if-then research on principle 9.
- The level of automation is best chosen per function, weighing reliability against the cost
  of a wrong act ([Parasuraman et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1109/3468.844354)), and
  reliance should follow what the automation has shown it can do
  ([Lee & See 2004](https://doi.org/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392)).

**What we don't claim**
- We don't promise a habit. An app cannot make one.
- The app moves no step on either road by itself. You decide what to cut down and what to hand
  over.

## Where this comes from

- **Routine dynamics.** A routine is a pattern and its performances, and each changes the
  other ([Feldman & Pentland 2003](https://doi.org/10.2307/3556620)).
- **After-action reviews.** A short look back after every performance: what was meant to
  happen, what did, what to change ([Keiser & Arthur 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000821)).
- **Implementation intentions.** If this, then that
  ([Gollwitzer 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493)).
- **Getting Things Done.** Capture on the go, clarify at a set time
  ([David Allen](https://gettingthingsdone.com/)).
- **Checklists.** Read an item, do it, then the next
  ([Degani & Wiener 1993](https://doi.org/10.1177/001872089303500209)).
- **Kaizen.** Small improvements from what is already done, made by the person doing it
  (Imai 1986; [Spear & Bowen 1999](https://hbr.org/1999/09/decoding-the-dna-of-the-toyota-production-system)).
- **Atomic Habits.** One simple process made a habit, then the next
  ([Clear 2018](https://jamesclear.com/atomic-habits)).
- **Do-nothing scripting.** Write a procedure as steps that only say what to do, then automate
  them one at a time ([Slimmon 2019](https://blog.danslimmon.com/2019/07/15/do-nothing-scripting-the-key-to-gradual-automation/)).
