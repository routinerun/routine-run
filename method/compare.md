# How it compares

The method is easiest to see beside the tools people already use. Each of them is good at
something, and several are good at what Routine Run does not try to do. This page compares
kinds of tool and the thinking behind them, principle by principle, not feature lists.
Product names are examples of a kind, not a ranking.

## The families

| Kind of tool | For example | What it is for | Where the method differs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Paper and plain checklists** | a laminated card, a note on the phone, the WHO surgical checklist | A fixed sequence that must never vary | The same list every time: no run, no record of how it went, nothing learned back ([3](method.md#built-by-running), [15](method.md#the-tune-and-the-playing)) |
| **To-do lists and task managers** | Todoist, Things, Apple Reminders, OmniFocus | What to do, as boxes to tick | They hold *what*. The method holds *how*, as ordered steps, and has no tick ([8](method.md#steps-not-checkboxes)) |
| **Day planners** | Structured, Tiimo | Laying out a day in time blocks, taking decisions away | They read your calendar and plan the day inside the app. The method is for winning time back, leaves decisions with you, and adds to the calendar and Reminders you already have, doing what they cannot, rather than replacing them ([11](method.md#dates-that-float), [17](method.md#who-it-is-for)) |
| **Routine and habit apps** | Routinery, Fabulous, Me+, Streaks | Guided routines, habits, streaks | Closest in shape. The method has no streaks or scores, learns the routine from each run, and steps can act ([3](method.md#built-by-running), [8](method.md#steps-not-checkboxes)) |
| **Process checklist software** | Process Street, Tallyfy | Template and run, for teams and companies | The same pair of template and run, built for an organisation's desk. The method is for one person's phone and wrist ([2](method.md#one-routine-many-runs)) |
| **Automation** | Apple Shortcuts, IFTTT, Zapier | Doing everything, with nobody there | Built to run with nobody there: a person can be asked in, but the whole procedure is written up front. The method gets quieter gradually: steps handed to a shortcut or a service one at a time, and steps that have become habit cut down by hand ([16](method.md#a-guide-not-an-autopilot), [18](method.md#the-guide-steps-back)) |
| **Incident platforms** | PagerDuty, incident.io, Rootly | Waking the right person, running the incident as an organisation | They page you and coordinate the team, with mobile apps to acknowledge and run playbooks. The method walks one person through their own first minutes, step by step, on the wrist ([9](method.md#if-then)) |
| **Agents that act for you** | assistants that carry out tasks on your behalf | Doing a task end to end | The same line as automation: the machine goes up to what cannot be undone, and stops there ([16](method.md#a-guide-not-an-autopilot)) |
| **Methods, not apps** | Getting Things Done, Kanban, after-action reviews | Ways of working | The method borrows from all three and puts them in one loop (see below) |

## Principle by principle

**1. The machine holds.** Every tool here holds something. A to-do app holds the list, a
planner holds the day, an automation tool holds the procedure. The difference is what is left
to you: in a to-do app, the order and the how; in an automation, nothing, until it fails.

**2. One routine, many runs.** Process checklist software such as Process Street and Tallyfy
has the same pair of template and run, and got there first. Most routine apps we looked at
keep a routine and a history of completions, not runs that go on for weeks with their own
values, several at once. A realtor's thirty clients are thirty runs of one routine; in a task
manager they are thirty copies of a project.

**3. Built by running.** Checklists are revised by an author away from the work. Habit apps
count completions of a fixed routine. Here what a run changed is answered Keep or Only here,
so the routine is written by doing it. We did not find a question like this at the end of a
run in Routinery, Fabulous or Streaks as of 26 September 2026.

**6 and 7. Only what is going; one step at a time.** Planners show the whole day as a
timeline; task managers show the whole list. Some routine apps, Routinery and Fabulous among
them, also lead one step at a time. The difference is the Lock Screen: a run's Next, Back,
Again and Later on the Lock Screen and in the Dynamic Island. Their Lock Screens show the current task and its timer; we did not find a documented Next or Later there from Routinery, Fabulous, Structured or Tiimo as of 26 September 2026. Streaks can pause a timed task from its Live Activity.

**8. Steps, not checkboxes.** Most habit apps are built on streaks and completion; task
managers on ticks and counts. The method has no score at all, only a count of questions to
answer.

**9. If this, then that step.** Automation tools are trigger and action in pure form, and do
it well. Among routine apps, Routinery can remind you to start when you arrive at or leave a place, and can start a routine from a link or a shortcut. The difference here is
that the trigger starts a *guided* run, and a step can act by itself when it is reached.

**10 and 11. Check-ins; dates that float.** Task managers repeat on a calendar ("every day") or from when you last ticked it, and their due dates stay fixed unless you move them. Project tools
have lags and dependencies, for a team's project plan. The method brings "asks until
answered" and "ten days after acceptance" to one person's routine.

**Planning the day: adding to the calendar, not replacing it.** Routine Run leaves the day
where it is and does what the calendar and Reminders cannot. They hold when something happens,
not the work leading up to it. Say a presentation is at 15:00. Three hours before, you do the
research. Two hours before, you prepare what you need. Half an hour before, you breathe and put
your thoughts in order. Five minutes before, you turn Zoom on and open what you need. A day
planner or a routine app lays this out at fixed times, and a calendar can hold each piece as a
separate reminder. Move the presentation even a little and every piece has to be moved by
hand. In Routine Run the plan hangs on the event. Put alerts on your own calendar event, three
hours, two hours, half an hour and five minutes before. Each alert that arrives can start a
routine, bring the run to the step for that stretch, or start another routine. The alerts are
counted from the event, so moving the event moves the whole plan. This needs an iPhone on
iOS 27 with the Routine Run shortcut set up; on iOS 26 it is not offered. Routine Run also
writes a run's moments and deadlines into your calendar and into Reminders. It notices when
you move or delete what it wrote, and asks whether that was for this once or for every time,
and never undoes it by itself. A tick in Reminders is read back as the step being done. The
plan lives in the tools that already hold your day, and the run is what walks through it.

**12 and 13. Put off; on the go and time set aside.** Getting Things Done is the method behind
this pair: capture, then clarify at a review. Email apps snooze; task managers defer to a
date. Here a step can be put off one place, to the end, to a time, to a place, or with the
deciding left for later, and an Inbox is a routine that stays open.

**14. Filter what arrives.** Apple's Focus is the mechanism, and any app can join it. The
method uses it to let the run through while holding the rest.

**15. The tune and the playing.** A checklist is a score, and should stay one where nothing
may vary: the WHO surgical checklist shows why. Automation is a player piano. The method sits
between: a tune, played live.

**16. A guide, not an autopilot.** A Shortcuts automation can ask before it runs; inside a shortcut, asking is an action you add by hand. The
method decides per step how much person is in it, and asks before the step that cannot be
undone.

## What others do better

- **Mac and web.** Structured and Tiimo run on a Mac and the web, and Routinery on a Mac; Routine Run does neither.
- **A day laid out for you.** For somebody who wants an app to arrange their day in blocks and
  become where the day lives, Structured and Tiimo are the better answer. Routine Run adds
  to the calendar and Reminders instead of replacing them.
- **Automation built in one go.** For something that must run silently at 3 a.m. from its
  first day, Shortcuts or Zapier is the right tool, and Routine Run can call a shortcut from a
  step. The method gets to quiet gradually: a step at a time handed to a machine, or cut down
  once it is habit.
- **Organisation-wide processes.** Process Street, Tallyfy and incident platforms have roles,
  permissions and dashboards for a company.

## Methods it borrows from

| Method | What it takes | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| Getting Things Done | Capture, then clarify at a set time | Capture lands as steps in a routine that stays open |
| Kanban | Limit what is in progress | Applied to runs in view, not cards on a board |
| After-action review | Review after every performance | Asked per change, with two cheap answers, at the end of every run |
| Checklists (read, then do) | One item, then the next | The list keeps its place when interrupted, and learns |
| Do-nothing scripting | Guide first, automate one step at a time | For people who do not write scripts |
| Implementation intentions | If this, then that | The phone keeps the if and watches for it |
| Kaizen | Small improvements from what is already done, by the person doing it | Asked per change at the end of every run: Keep or Only here |
| Atomic Habits | One simple process made habit, then the next; habit stacking | The step before is the cue; a learned step shrinks to a line; no tracker, no streak |
